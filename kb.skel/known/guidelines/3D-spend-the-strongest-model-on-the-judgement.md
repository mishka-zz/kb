The model is picked by what the agent's output decides, and not by how large the
task looks or by which step of [[2B]] it fills. There are three weights.

- **A decision the project will stand on** - an architecture, where a boundary
  falls, what a milestone takes, a claim the record will cite as settled.
  Nothing downstream re-derives it and a wrong one is paid for over months. This
  is the one place for the most capable model available.
- **Work done on its own** - a design argued for a bounded change, a clean
  reader, an adversarial tester who has to find the specimens that break the
  mechanism. It wants judgement and independence, and a strong model at high
  reasoning effort gives both; the most capable one adds cost here and little
  else.
- **The mechanical** - running a prepared script, gathering citations out of
  somebody else's tree, reading files for facts. A wrong answer is caught by
  opening the file, and a cheaper model does it.

Reading a client's source and mapping it against our own model is long and
mechanical; deciding what the mismatch means for a layer is short and decides a
milestone. Both halves' reports are checked against the code before being acted
on, as [[1B]] requires, and that check finds errors in each - the ordinary
outcome, and why the reading half is the recoverable half.

An agent runs on the model its call names, or else on the one its type's
definition pins, or else on the session's. So the model is named in the call and
never left to the type: on the record it came from a design argument went to a
planning agent with no model named, and the rule that covered it had not been
applied.

Even from the strongest model the output is an expert's opinion and not an
instruction. It is argued with, checked against the code, and the decision stays
with whoever acts on it ([[2E]]).
